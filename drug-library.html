Anticoagulants

Heparin
MOA:
Indirect anticoagulant that potentiates antithrombin III, which inhibits thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa.
Dose for Loading for CPB:
Common adult loading dose range is about 300 to 400 units/kg IV, with dosing then confirmed by ACT or other anticoagulation monitoring per protocol.
Metabolism:
Primarily cleared by hepatic metabolism and the reticuloendothelial system; clearance is dose dependent.
T1/2:
Approximately 0.5 to 2 hours, depending on dose.
Bivalirudin
MOA:
Direct thrombin inhibitor that binds thrombin and inhibits thrombin-mediated fibrin formation, platelet activation, and clot propagation.
Dose for Loading for CPB:
Common adult on-pump CPB regimen: 1 mg/kg IV loading bolus, with 50 mg added to the pump prime per protocol.
Continuing Dose While on Bypass:
Common adult on-pump CPB infusion: 2.5 mg/kg/hr while anticoagulation is required, titrated to ACT or institutional monitoring targets.
Metabolism:
Primarily metabolized by proteolytic cleavage, with partial renal clearance. Clearance is reduced when renal function is impaired.
T1/2:
Approximately 25 minutes in patients with normal renal function; prolonged with renal impairment.

Reversals for Anticoagulants

Protamine
MOA:
Strongly cationic protein that binds acidic unfractionated heparin to form a stable inactive salt complex, neutralizing the anticoagulant effect of both drugs.
Dose and Ratio to Heparin:
Common reversal ratio is 1 mg protamine per 100 units of unfractionated heparin. Dose should be adjusted for time since heparin administration and guided by ACT, aPTT, or institutional protocol.
Metabolism:
The metabolic fate of the heparin-protamine complex is not fully defined; it may be partially metabolized or degraded by fibrinolysin, which can contribute to heparin rebound.
T1/2:
Short half-life, approximately 7 to 10 minutes, so repeat dosing may be needed when heparin effect persists or rebounds.
Bivalirudin Reversal / Bleeding Management
Direct Antidote:
No specific direct reversal agent is available. Management relies on stopping bivalirudin, allowing drug clearance, and supporting hemostasis.
Modified Ultrafiltration:
May be used after CPB to help reduce circulating bivalirudin effect and manage excessive bleeding in select cases.
Hemodialysis:
Bivalirudin is dialyzable and hemodialysis may be considered when anticoagulant effect is prolonged, especially with renal impairment.
Recombinant Factor VIIa:
May be considered as rescue therapy for severe refractory bleeding or coagulopathy after bivalirudin anticoagulation, guided by institutional protocol.
FFP:
Adjunctive hemostatic support to replace coagulation factors during balanced resuscitation; it does not directly neutralize bivalirudin.
Cryoprecipitate:
Adjunctive support for fibrinogen replacement when fibrinogen is low or bleeding is consistent with hypofibrinogenemia.
Platelets:
Adjunctive support for thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction after CPB when clinically indicated.

Vasopressors

Vasopressin
MOA:
Non-catecholamine vasopressor that stimulates vascular V1 receptors, causing vasoconstriction and increased systemic vascular resistance.
Dose:
Common adult vasodilatory shock range: 0.01 to 0.1 units/min continuous IV infusion. Many protocols use 0.03 to 0.04 units/min as a fixed adjunct dose.
Angiotensin II
MOA:
Activates angiotensin II type 1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle, producing potent vasoconstriction and aldosterone-mediated sodium and water retention.
Dose:
Start 20 ng/kg/min continuous IV infusion through a central line when possible. Titrate by up to 15 ng/kg/min every 5 minutes; do not exceed 80 ng/kg/min during the first 3 hours or 40 ng/kg/min for maintenance.
Phenylephrine
MOA:
Selective alpha-1 adrenergic agonist that increases systemic vascular resistance through arterial and venous vasoconstriction, usually with minimal direct beta activity.
Dose:
Bolus for anesthesia-associated hypotension: 50 to 250 mcg IV. Infusion: 0.5 to 1.4 mcg/kg/min for anesthesia-associated hypotension, or 0.5 to 6 mcg/kg/min for septic or other vasodilatory shock, titrated to blood pressure goal.
Dilution:
Phenylephrine 10 mg/mL must be diluted before IV bolus or infusion. For a common 100 mcg/mL bolus concentration, add 1 mL of 10 mg/mL phenylephrine to 99 mL compatible diluent.
Norepinephrine
MOA:
Predominantly alpha-1 adrenergic agonist with beta-1 activity, increasing systemic vascular resistance and supporting contractility.
Dose:
Common adult starting range: 0.05 to 0.1 mcg/kg/min or 8 to 12 mcg/min continuous IV infusion, titrated to effect. Typical maintenance is often 2 to 4 mcg/min or about 0.05 to 0.4 mcg/kg/min.
Epinephrine
MOA:
Endogenous catecholamine with alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 agonist activity, increasing contractility, heart rate, and vascular tone in a dose-dependent manner.
Dose:
For septic shock-associated hypotension: 0.05 to 2 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion, titrated to MAP or blood pressure goal. Adjust in increments of 0.05 to 0.2 mcg/kg/min every 10 to 15 minutes as needed.
Dopamine
MOA:
Dose-dependent catecholamine: lower doses stimulate dopaminergic receptors, intermediate doses stimulate beta-1 receptors, and higher doses stimulate alpha-1 receptors to increase vascular tone.
Dose:
Start 2 to 5 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion and titrate by 5 to 10 mcg/kg/min based on hemodynamic response and tolerability. Maximum recommended dose is 50 mcg/kg/min.

Inotropes

Milrinone
MOA:
Phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor that increases intracellular cAMP, improving myocardial contractility and relaxation while producing systemic and pulmonary vasodilation.
Dose:
Common adult regimen: 50 mcg/kg IV loading dose over 10 minutes, then 0.375 to 0.75 mcg/kg/min continuous infusion. Loading dose is often omitted or reduced when hypotension is a concern.
Dobutamine
MOA:
Synthetic catecholamine with predominant beta-1 agonist activity that increases contractility and cardiac output, with variable beta-2 and alpha-1 effects on vascular tone.
Dose:
Common adult infusion range: start 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg/min and titrate to effect; usual effective range is 2 to 20 mcg/kg/min, with higher doses up to 40 mcg/kg/min used rarely.

Vasodilators

Nitroglycerin
MOA:
Nitrate vasodilator converted to nitric oxide, increasing cyclic GMP and relaxing vascular smooth muscle. Venodilation predominates at lower doses, with arterial dilation at higher doses.
Dose:
Start 5 mcg/min continuous IV infusion with non-adsorptive tubing. Increase by 5 mcg/min every 3 to 5 minutes until response or 20 mcg/min, then increase by 10 to 20 mcg/min as needed and tolerated.
Sodium Nitroprusside
MOA:
Direct arterial and venous vasodilator that releases nitric oxide, reducing systemic vascular resistance, preload, and afterload.
Dose:
Start 0.3 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion and titrate to blood pressure response. Usual range is about 0.5 to 10 mcg/kg/min; maximum-rate infusions should be brief because of cyanide and thiocyanate toxicity risk.
Inhaled Epoprostenol
MOA:
Prostacyclin analog that increases cyclic AMP in pulmonary vascular smooth muscle, producing pulmonary vasodilation and inhibiting platelet aggregation.
Dose:
Protocol-driven inhaled dose range is commonly 5 to 50 ng/kg/min, often started at 50 ng/kg/min for severe pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular failure and weaned to the lowest effective dose.

Antihypertensives

Beta blockers
Esmolol
MOA:
Ultra-short acting, cardioselective beta-1 adrenergic blocker that decreases heart rate, contractility, and AV nodal conduction.
Dose:
Gradual control: 500 mcg/kg IV bolus over 1 minute, then 50 mcg/kg/min infusion for 4 minutes. Titrate by repeating bolus and increasing infusion by 50 mcg/kg/min steps as needed; maximum recommended infusion is 300 mcg/kg/min.
Metoprolol
MOA:
Cardioselective beta-1 adrenergic blocker that reduces heart rate, myocardial contractility, cardiac output, and AV nodal conduction.
Dose:
Common IV regimen: 5 mg IV over several minutes, repeated every 2 minutes up to 3 doses for a total of 15 mg as tolerated, with transition to oral metoprolol when appropriate.
Labetalol
MOA:
Combined alpha-1 and nonselective beta adrenergic blocker that lowers blood pressure by reducing systemic vascular resistance while limiting reflex tachycardia.
Dose:
Repeated IV bolus regimen: 20 mg IV over 2 minutes, then 40 mg or 80 mg at 10-minute intervals until target blood pressure or a total dose of 300 mg. Continuous infusion may be used per protocol after bolus dosing.
Calcium Channel blockers
Clevidipine
MOA:
Short-acting dihydropyridine L-type calcium channel blocker that relaxes arterial smooth muscle, lowering systemic vascular resistance with little direct effect on venous capacitance vessels.
Dose:
Start 1 to 2 mg/hr continuous IV infusion. Initially, the dose may be doubled about every 90 seconds; as blood pressure approaches goal, titrate by smaller increments every 5 to 10 minutes. Most patients respond around 4 to 6 mg/hr; most maximum doses are 16 mg/hr or less, with limited short-term experience up to 32 mg/hr.
Nicardipine
MOA:
Dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker that inhibits calcium influx into vascular smooth muscle, producing arterial vasodilation and reduced systemic vascular resistance.
Dose:
Common adult IV infusion regimen: start 5 mg/hr and increase by 2.5 mg/hr every 5 to 15 minutes until target blood pressure is reached. Maximum dose is 15 mg/hr; once controlled, consider reducing to about 3 mg/hr for maintenance per protocol.

Inhaled Anesthetics

Isoflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic that produces hypnosis, amnesia, and immobility through multiple CNS targets, including potentiation of inhibitory signaling and reduced excitatory transmission.
Dose:
Induction: inspired concentrations of 1.5% to 3% usually produce surgical anesthesia in 7 to 10 minutes. Maintenance: 1% to 2.5% with nitrous oxide; add about 0.5% to 1% when used with oxygen alone.
Cardiac Considerations:
Produces dose-dependent hypotension and negative inotropy, largely from reduced systemic vascular resistance. Rapid increases can raise heart rate and sympathetic tone; beta blockers and calcium channel blockers may exaggerate hypotension or myocardial depression.
Desflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic with low blood-gas solubility that produces general anesthesia through broad modulation of inhibitory and excitatory CNS ion channels.
Dose:
Maintenance: surgical anesthesia in adults may be maintained with 2.5% to 8.5% desflurane, with or without nitrous oxide. Dose is individualized to age, co-administered anesthetics, and clinical response.
Cardiac Considerations:
Increasing concentrations cause dose-dependent blood pressure reduction. Concentrations above 1 MAC or rapid increases can increase heart rate, blood pressure, catecholamines, and sympathetic activity, so tachycardia may not reliably indicate light anesthesia.
Sevoflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic that supports hypnosis, amnesia, and immobility through multiple CNS receptor effects, including enhanced inhibitory signaling and reduced excitatory neurotransmission.
Dose:
Maintenance: surgical levels of anesthesia are commonly achieved with 0.5% to 3% sevoflurane, with or without nitrous oxide. Dose should be titrated to age, MAC modifiers, and clinical response.
Cardiac Considerations:
Dose-related cardiac depressant that can reduce blood pressure and cardiac output, mainly through reduced systemic vascular resistance. It generally does not increase heart rate below 2 MAC and has arrhythmogenic interaction with epinephrine similar to isoflurane.
