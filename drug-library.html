Anticoagulants

Heparin
MOA:
Indirect anticoagulant that potentiates antithrombin III, which inhibits thrombin (factor IIa) and factor Xa.
Dose for Loading for CPB:
Common adult loading dose range is about 300 to 400 units/kg IV, with dosing then confirmed by ACT or other anticoagulation monitoring per protocol.
Metabolism:
Primarily cleared by hepatic metabolism and the reticuloendothelial system; clearance is dose dependent.
T1/2:
Approximately 0.5 to 2 hours, depending on dose.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Bleeding, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia, thrombocytopenia, hypersensitivity, hyperkalemia, and aminotransferase elevation.
Contraindications:
Active uncontrolled bleeding, severe thrombocytopenia, hypersensitivity to heparin or pork products, or inability to perform appropriate coagulation monitoring.
Cardiac Considerations:
Monitor closely for surgical bleeding, tamponade physiology, HIT-associated thrombosis, and hyperkalemia-related conduction changes.
Bivalirudin
MOA:
Direct thrombin inhibitor that binds thrombin and inhibits thrombin-mediated fibrin formation, platelet activation, and clot propagation.
Dose for Loading for CPB:
Common adult on-pump CPB regimen: 1 mg/kg IV loading bolus, with 50 mg added to the pump prime per protocol.
Continuing Dose While on Bypass:
Common adult on-pump CPB infusion: 2.5 mg/kg/hr while anticoagulation is required, titrated to ACT or institutional monitoring targets.
Metabolism:
Primarily metabolized by proteolytic cleavage, with partial renal clearance. Clearance is reduced when renal function is impaired.
T1/2:
Approximately 25 minutes in patients with normal renal function; prolonged with renal impairment.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Bleeding, hypotension, back pain, nausea, headache, and rare hypersensitivity or thrombosis events.
Contraindications:
Significant active bleeding or hypersensitivity to bivalirudin or formulation components.
Cardiac Considerations:
No direct reversal agent exists; renal impairment can prolong anticoagulation and increase risk of postoperative bleeding or tamponade.
Argatroban
MOA:
Direct thrombin inhibitor that reversibly binds thrombin and inhibits fibrin formation, platelet activation, and clot propagation.
Dose for HIT:
Common adult initial infusion is 2 mcg/kg/min IV in patients without hepatic impairment, titrated to aPTT per protocol. Lower initial doses are used with hepatic impairment or critical illness.
Dose for PCI:
Labeled PCI regimen includes 350 mcg/kg IV bolus over 3 to 5 minutes plus 25 mcg/kg/min infusion, with ACT checked 5 to 10 minutes after bolus completion.
Metabolism:
Primarily hepatic metabolism. Dose reduction and close monitoring are important with hepatic impairment.
T1/2:
Approximately 39 to 51 minutes in patients without hepatic impairment; may be prolonged with hepatic dysfunction.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Bleeding, hypotension, fever, diarrhea, sepsis, cardiac arrest, and hypersensitivity reactions have been reported.
Contraindications:
Major bleeding or history of hypersensitivity to argatroban or formulation components.
Cardiac Considerations:
Not approved for CPB/bypass anticoagulation. No specific reversal agent exists; monitor ACT/aPTT per use case and use caution when hepatic dysfunction may prolong effect.

Reversals for Anticoagulants

Protamine
MOA:
Strongly cationic protein that binds acidic unfractionated heparin to form a stable inactive salt complex, neutralizing the anticoagulant effect of both drugs.
Dose and Ratio to Heparin:
Common reversal ratio is 1 mg protamine per 100 units of unfractionated heparin. Dose should be adjusted for time since heparin administration and guided by ACT, aPTT, or institutional protocol.
Metabolism:
The metabolic fate of the heparin-protamine complex is not fully defined; it may be partially metabolized or degraded by fibrinolysin, which can contribute to heparin rebound.
T1/2:
Short half-life, approximately 7 to 10 minutes, so repeat dosing may be needed when heparin effect persists or rebounds.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, bradycardia, flushing, dyspnea, pulmonary hypertension, bronchoconstriction, and anaphylactoid reactions.
Contraindications:
Known hypersensitivity; use heightened caution with prior protamine exposure, NPH insulin use, fish allergy, or prior vasectomy.
Cardiac Considerations:
Give slowly and monitor for abrupt vasodilation, right ventricular strain from pulmonary vasoconstriction, and heparin rebound.
Bivalirudin Reversal / Bleeding Management
Direct Antidote:
No specific direct reversal agent is available. Management relies on stopping bivalirudin, allowing drug clearance, and supporting hemostasis.
Modified Ultrafiltration:
May be used after CPB to help reduce circulating bivalirudin effect and manage excessive bleeding in select cases.
Hemodialysis:
Bivalirudin is dialyzable and hemodialysis may be considered when anticoagulant effect is prolonged, especially with renal impairment.
Recombinant Factor VIIa:
May be considered as rescue therapy for severe refractory bleeding or coagulopathy after bivalirudin anticoagulation, guided by institutional protocol.
FFP:
Adjunctive hemostatic support to replace coagulation factors during balanced resuscitation; it does not directly neutralize bivalirudin.
Cryoprecipitate:
Adjunctive support for fibrinogen replacement when fibrinogen is low or bleeding is consistent with hypofibrinogenemia.
Platelets:
Adjunctive support for thrombocytopenia or platelet dysfunction after CPB when clinically indicated.

Vasopressors

Vasopressin
MOA:
Non-catecholamine vasopressor that stimulates vascular V1 receptors, causing vasoconstriction and increased systemic vascular resistance.
Dose:
Common adult vasodilatory shock range: 0.01 to 0.1 units/min continuous IV infusion. Many protocols use 0.03 to 0.04 units/min as a fixed adjunct dose.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Bradycardia, atrial fibrillation, myocardial ischemia, decreased cardiac output, mesenteric or peripheral ischemia, hyponatremia, and skin ischemia.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to vasopressin or formulation components.
Cardiac Considerations:
Useful in catecholamine-resistant vasoplegia, but excessive vasoconstriction can worsen coronary, mesenteric, or limb perfusion.
Desmopressin
MOA:
V2 agonist that increases endothelial release of von Willebrand factor and factor VIII, improving platelet adhesion.
Dose:
Hemostatic dose: 0.3 mcg/kg IV over 15 to 30 minutes; restrict free water and monitor sodium.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hyponatremia, water retention, headache, flushing, nausea, hypotension, tachycardia, seizures, and rare thrombosis.
Contraindications:
Hyponatremia or high hyponatremia risk, CrCl below 50 mL/min, hypersensitivity, and active or high-risk thrombotic disease.
Cardiac Considerations:
May reduce platelet-related bleeding after CPB; monitor sodium, fluid balance, ischemia, and thrombosis risk.
Angiotensin II
MOA:
Activates angiotensin II type 1 receptors on vascular smooth muscle, producing potent vasoconstriction and aldosterone-mediated sodium and water retention.
Dose:
Start 20 ng/kg/min continuous IV infusion through a central line when possible. Titrate by up to 15 ng/kg/min every 5 minutes; do not exceed 80 ng/kg/min during the first 3 hours or 40 ng/kg/min for maintenance.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Thromboembolic events, thrombocytopenia, tachycardia, fungal infection, delirium, acidosis, hyperglycemia, and peripheral ischemia.
Contraindications:
No labeled contraindications, but concurrent venous thromboembolism prophylaxis is recommended unless contraindicated.
Cardiac Considerations:
Potent afterload support for vasodilatory shock; watch for excessive vasoconstriction, ventricular strain, and thrombotic risk.
Phenylephrine
MOA:
Selective alpha-1 adrenergic agonist that increases systemic vascular resistance through arterial and venous vasoconstriction, usually with minimal direct beta activity.
Dose:
Bolus for anesthesia-associated hypotension: 50 to 250 mcg IV. Infusion: 0.5 to 1.4 mcg/kg/min for anesthesia-associated hypotension, or 0.5 to 6 mcg/kg/min for septic or other vasodilatory shock, titrated to blood pressure goal.
Dilution:
Phenylephrine 10 mg/mL must be diluted before IV bolus or infusion. For a common 100 mcg/mL bolus concentration, add 1 mL of 10 mg/mL phenylephrine to 99 mL compatible diluent.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypertension, reflex bradycardia, headache, nausea, extravasation injury, and peripheral or visceral ischemia.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to phenylephrine or formulation components; avoid use with severe hypertension or ventricular tachycardia unless benefits outweigh risk.
Cardiac Considerations:
Increases afterload and may lower heart rate or cardiac output, so use carefully in ventricular dysfunction or fixed outflow obstruction.
Norepinephrine
MOA:
Predominantly alpha-1 adrenergic agonist with beta-1 activity, increasing systemic vascular resistance and supporting contractility.
Dose:
Common adult starting range: 0.05 to 0.1 mcg/kg/min or 8 to 12 mcg/min continuous IV infusion, titrated to effect. Typical maintenance is often 2 to 4 mcg/min or about 0.05 to 0.4 mcg/kg/min.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypertension, tachyarrhythmias, bradycardia, anxiety, headache, tissue necrosis with extravasation, and peripheral ischemia.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to norepinephrine or formulation components; avoid in hypotension from blood-volume deficit except as an emergency bridge.
Cardiac Considerations:
Raises coronary perfusion pressure but can increase afterload, myocardial oxygen demand, and arrhythmia risk at higher doses.
Epinephrine
MOA:
Endogenous catecholamine with alpha-1, beta-1, and beta-2 agonist activity, increasing contractility, heart rate, and vascular tone in a dose-dependent manner.
Dose:
For septic shock-associated hypotension: 0.05 to 2 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion, titrated to MAP or blood pressure goal. Adjust in increments of 0.05 to 0.2 mcg/kg/min every 10 to 15 minutes as needed.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Tachycardia, ventricular arrhythmias, hypertension, myocardial ischemia, hyperglycemia, lactic acidosis, tremor, and peripheral ischemia.
Contraindications:
No absolute contraindication in emergency use; avoid elective use with hypersensitivity or when tachyarrhythmias are uncontrolled.
Cardiac Considerations:
Strong inotrope and pressor that can increase cardiac output but also increases heart rate, oxygen demand, and arrhythmia risk.
Dopamine
MOA:
Dose-dependent catecholamine: lower doses stimulate dopaminergic receptors, intermediate doses stimulate beta-1 receptors, and higher doses stimulate alpha-1 receptors to increase vascular tone.
Dose:
Start 2 to 5 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion and titrate by 5 to 10 mcg/kg/min based on hemodynamic response and tolerability. Maximum recommended dose is 50 mcg/kg/min.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Tachycardia, ectopy, angina, hypertension, hypotension, nausea, headache, extravasation injury, and peripheral ischemia.
Contraindications:
Pheochromocytoma or uncorrected tachyarrhythmias such as ventricular fibrillation or ventricular tachycardia.
Cardiac Considerations:
Can improve contractility and pressure but is relatively arrhythmogenic and may worsen myocardial oxygen balance.

Inotropes

Milrinone
MOA:
Phosphodiesterase-3 inhibitor that increases intracellular cAMP, improving myocardial contractility and relaxation while producing systemic and pulmonary vasodilation.
Dose:
Common adult regimen: 50 mcg/kg IV loading dose over 10 minutes, then 0.375 to 0.75 mcg/kg/min continuous infusion. Loading dose is often omitted or reduced when hypotension is a concern.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Ventricular arrhythmias, supraventricular arrhythmias, hypotension, angina or chest pain, headache, hypokalemia, thrombocytopenia, and rare torsades de pointes.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to milrinone or formulation components.
Cardiac Considerations:
Improves contractility and pulmonary vascular tone but may cause hypotension; reduce dose in renal impairment and monitor rhythm closely.
Dobutamine
MOA:
Synthetic catecholamine with predominant beta-1 agonist activity that increases contractility and cardiac output, with variable beta-2 and alpha-1 effects on vascular tone.
Dose:
Common adult infusion range: start 0.5 to 1 mcg/kg/min and titrate to effect; usual effective range is 2 to 20 mcg/kg/min, with higher doses up to 40 mcg/kg/min used rarely.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Tachycardia, ectopy, hypertension, hypotension, angina, dyspnea, headache, nausea, and infusion-site phlebitis.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to dobutamine or sulfites; idiopathic hypertrophic subaortic stenosis.
Cardiac Considerations:
Boosts contractility and cardiac output but can provoke tachyarrhythmias and increase myocardial oxygen consumption.

Vasodilators

Nitroglycerin
MOA:
Nitrate vasodilator converted to nitric oxide, increasing cyclic GMP and relaxing vascular smooth muscle. Venodilation predominates at lower doses, with arterial dilation at higher doses.
Dose:
Start 5 mcg/min continuous IV infusion with non-adsorptive tubing. Increase by 5 mcg/min every 3 to 5 minutes until response or 20 mcg/min, then increase by 10 to 20 mcg/min as needed and tolerated.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Headache, flushing, hypotension, reflex tachycardia, dizziness, nausea, methemoglobinemia, and tolerance with prolonged exposure.
Contraindications:
Use with PDE-5 inhibitors or riociguat, severe anemia, increased intracranial pressure, or hypersensitivity to nitrates.
Cardiac Considerations:
Reduces preload and treats coronary vasospasm, but hypotension can compromise coronary perfusion in preload-dependent states.
Sodium Nitroprusside
MOA:
Direct arterial and venous vasodilator that releases nitric oxide, reducing systemic vascular resistance, preload, and afterload.
Dose:
Start 0.3 mcg/kg/min continuous IV infusion and titrate to blood pressure response. Usual range is about 0.5 to 10 mcg/kg/min; maximum-rate infusions should be brief because of cyanide and thiocyanate toxicity risk.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Excessive hypotension, reflex tachycardia, cyanide toxicity, thiocyanate toxicity, nausea, vomiting, diaphoresis, and metabolic acidosis.
Contraindications:
Compensatory hypertension such as arteriovenous shunting or coarctation, Leber optic atrophy, or tobacco amblyopia.
Cardiac Considerations:
Rapidly lowers preload and afterload; avoid prolonged high-dose use and watch for hypotension, coronary steal, and acidosis.
Inhaled Epoprostenol
MOA:
Prostacyclin analog that increases cyclic AMP in pulmonary vascular smooth muscle, producing pulmonary vasodilation and inhibiting platelet aggregation.
Dose:
Protocol-driven inhaled dose range is commonly 5 to 50 ng/kg/min, often started at 50 ng/kg/min for severe pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular failure and weaned to the lowest effective dose.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, flushing, headache, jaw pain, platelet inhibition, bleeding risk, and rebound pulmonary hypertension if abruptly stopped.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to epoprostenol; avoid when pulmonary edema is suspected from pulmonary veno-occlusive disease.
Cardiac Considerations:
Selective pulmonary vasodilation can support right ventricular function, but systemic spillover or abrupt interruption can destabilize pressure and oxygenation.

Antihypertensives

Beta blockers
Esmolol
MOA:
Ultra-short acting, cardioselective beta-1 adrenergic blocker that decreases heart rate, contractility, and AV nodal conduction.
Dose:
Gradual control: 500 mcg/kg IV bolus over 1 minute, then 50 mcg/kg/min infusion for 4 minutes. Titrate by repeating bolus and increasing infusion by 50 mcg/kg/min steps as needed; maximum recommended infusion is 300 mcg/kg/min.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, bradycardia, heart block, bronchospasm, nausea, dizziness, and infusion-site reactions.
Contraindications:
Severe sinus bradycardia, heart block greater than first degree, cardiogenic shock, decompensated heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, and hypersensitivity.
Cardiac Considerations:
Short half-life makes it useful for titratable rate control, but excessive beta blockade can reduce contractility and impair separation from bypass.
Metoprolol
MOA:
Cardioselective beta-1 adrenergic blocker that reduces heart rate, myocardial contractility, cardiac output, and AV nodal conduction.
Dose:
Common IV regimen: 5 mg IV over several minutes, repeated every 2 minutes up to 3 doses for a total of 15 mg as tolerated, with transition to oral metoprolol when appropriate.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Bradycardia, hypotension, heart block, worsening heart failure, bronchospasm, fatigue, dizziness, nausea, and depression.
Contraindications:
Severe bradycardia, heart block greater than first degree, cardiogenic shock, decompensated heart failure, sick sinus syndrome without pacing, and hypersensitivity.
Cardiac Considerations:
Useful for rate control and ischemia reduction, but monitor for low output, AV block, and difficulty supporting contractility post-CPB.
Labetalol
MOA:
Combined alpha-1 and nonselective beta adrenergic blocker that lowers blood pressure by reducing systemic vascular resistance while limiting reflex tachycardia.
Dose:
Repeated IV bolus regimen: 20 mg IV over 2 minutes, then 40 mg or 80 mg at 10-minute intervals until target blood pressure or a total dose of 300 mg. Continuous infusion may be used per protocol after bolus dosing.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, bradycardia, heart block, dizziness, nausea, bronchospasm, scalp tingling, and rare hepatotoxicity.
Contraindications:
Bronchial asthma, overt cardiac failure, heart block greater than first degree, cardiogenic shock, severe bradycardia, and hypersensitivity.
Cardiac Considerations:
Lowers blood pressure with less reflex tachycardia, but beta blockade can worsen low-output states or conduction disease.
Calcium Channel blockers
Clevidipine
MOA:
Short-acting dihydropyridine L-type calcium channel blocker that relaxes arterial smooth muscle, lowering systemic vascular resistance with little direct effect on venous capacitance vessels.
Dose:
Start 1 to 2 mg/hr continuous IV infusion. Initially, the dose may be doubled about every 90 seconds; as blood pressure approaches goal, titrate by smaller increments every 5 to 10 minutes. Most patients respond around 4 to 6 mg/hr; most maximum doses are 16 mg/hr or less, with limited short-term experience up to 32 mg/hr.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, reflex tachycardia, nausea, headache, vomiting, atrial fibrillation, and infusion-related lipid load.
Contraindications:
Allergy to soybeans, soy products, eggs, egg products, or defective lipid metabolism.
Cardiac Considerations:
Rapid arterial afterload reduction can help hypertension, but avoid overshoot hypotension and monitor tachycardia or worsening aortic stenosis physiology.
Nicardipine
MOA:
Dihydropyridine calcium channel blocker that inhibits calcium influx into vascular smooth muscle, producing arterial vasodilation and reduced systemic vascular resistance.
Dose:
Common adult IV infusion regimen: start 5 mg/hr and increase by 2.5 mg/hr every 5 to 15 minutes until target blood pressure is reached. Maximum dose is 15 mg/hr; once controlled, consider reducing to about 3 mg/hr for maintenance per protocol.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Headache, nausea, vomiting, hypotension, reflex tachycardia, flushing, edema, and infusion-site reactions.
Contraindications:
Advanced aortic stenosis or hypersensitivity to nicardipine.
Cardiac Considerations:
Afterload reduction is titratable, but reflex tachycardia and reduced diastolic pressure may worsen ischemia or fixed-output lesions.

Inhaled Anesthetics

Isoflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic that produces hypnosis, amnesia, and immobility through multiple CNS targets, including potentiation of inhibitory signaling and reduced excitatory transmission.
Dose:
Induction: inspired concentrations of 1.5% to 3% usually produce surgical anesthesia in 7 to 10 minutes. Maintenance: 1% to 2.5% with nitrous oxide; add about 0.5% to 1% when used with oxygen alone.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, respiratory depression, arrhythmias, nausea, vomiting, shivering, laryngospasm, hyperkalemia in susceptible patients, and malignant hyperthermia.
Contraindications:
Known or suspected genetic susceptibility to malignant hyperthermia or hypersensitivity to isoflurane or other halogenated anesthetics.
Cardiac Considerations:
Produces dose-dependent hypotension and negative inotropy, largely from reduced systemic vascular resistance. Rapid increases can raise heart rate and sympathetic tone; beta blockers and calcium channel blockers may exaggerate hypotension or myocardial depression.
Desflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic with low blood-gas solubility that produces general anesthesia through broad modulation of inhibitory and excitatory CNS ion channels.
Dose:
Maintenance: surgical anesthesia in adults may be maintained with 2.5% to 8.5% desflurane, with or without nitrous oxide. Dose is individualized to age, co-administered anesthetics, and clinical response.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, respiratory irritation, coughing, laryngospasm, apnea, nausea, vomiting, tachycardia or hypertension with rapid increases, and malignant hyperthermia.
Contraindications:
Known or suspected malignant hyperthermia susceptibility, hypersensitivity to halogenated anesthetics, or use for mask induction in pediatric patients.
Cardiac Considerations:
Increasing concentrations cause dose-dependent blood pressure reduction. Concentrations above 1 MAC or rapid increases can increase heart rate, blood pressure, catecholamines, and sympathetic activity, so tachycardia may not reliably indicate light anesthesia.
Sevoflurane
MOA:
Volatile halogenated anesthetic that supports hypnosis, amnesia, and immobility through multiple CNS receptor effects, including enhanced inhibitory signaling and reduced excitatory neurotransmission.
Dose:
Maintenance: surgical levels of anesthesia are commonly achieved with 0.5% to 3% sevoflurane, with or without nitrous oxide. Dose should be titrated to age, MAC modifiers, and clinical response.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, respiratory depression, nausea, vomiting, agitation, cough, laryngospasm, bradycardia, hyperkalemia in susceptible patients, and malignant hyperthermia.
Contraindications:
Known or suspected malignant hyperthermia susceptibility or hypersensitivity to sevoflurane or other halogenated anesthetics.
Cardiac Considerations:
Dose-related cardiac depressant that can reduce blood pressure and cardiac output, mainly through reduced systemic vascular resistance. It generally does not increase heart rate below 2 MAC and has arrhythmogenic interaction with epinephrine similar to isoflurane.

Antifibrinolytics

TXA
MOA:
Synthetic lysine analog that blocks plasminogen binding to fibrin, reducing plasmin formation and fibrinolysis.
Dose:
Common CPB range: 10 to 30 mg/kg IV load, 1 to 16 mg/kg/hr infusion, and 1 to 2 mg/kg in the pump prime per protocol.
T1/2:
About 2 hours; primarily renal elimination.
Contraindications:
Active intravascular clotting, subarachnoid hemorrhage, hypersensitivity, and neuraxial administration.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Seizures, thromboembolism, hypotension with rapid IV dosing, visual symptoms, nausea, diarrhea, and hypersensitivity.
EACA
MOA:
Synthetic lysine analog that inhibits plasminogen activation and plasmin activity, stabilizing fibrin clot.
Dose:
Common CPB range: 100 to 150 mg/kg IV load, then 10 to 30 mg/kg/hr or 1 g/hr infusion; some protocols add pump-prime dosing.
T1/2:
About 2 hours; primarily renal elimination.
Contraindications:
Active intravascular clotting unless due to primary fibrinolysis; hypersensitivity; use caution with renal impairment.
Adverse Drug Reactions:
Hypotension, bradycardia, thrombosis, renal impairment, myopathy, seizures, GI upset, edema, and injection-site reactions.

Paralytics

Cisatracurium
MOA:
Nondepolarizing benzylisoquinolinium neuromuscular blocker; competitively blocks acetylcholine at nicotinic receptors.
Dose:
0.15 to 0.2 mg/kg IV intubation; 0.03 mg/kg IV maintenance or 1 to 3 mcg/kg/min infusion, titrated to TOF.
Half-life:
About 22 to 29 minutes.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to cisatracurium; avoid benzyl-alcohol multidose vials in neonates/low-birth-weight infants.
Cardiac Considerations:
Minimal dose-related HR/MAP effect; does not blunt vagal or anesthetic-related bradycardia.
Rocuronium
MOA:
Nondepolarizing aminosteroid neuromuscular blocker; competitively blocks acetylcholine at nicotinic receptors.
Dose:
0.6 mg/kg IV intubation; 0.6 to 1.2 mg/kg IV RSI; 0.1 to 0.2 mg/kg IV maintenance or 4 to 16 mcg/kg/min infusion, titrated to TOF.
Half-life:
About 1.4 to 2.4 hours; effect may be prolonged with hepatic disease.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to rocuronium or other neuromuscular blocking agents.
Cardiac Considerations:
Generally hemodynamically stable; prolonged circulation time delays onset, so avoid reflex dose escalation.
Vecuronium
MOA:
Nondepolarizing aminosteroid neuromuscular blocker; competitively blocks acetylcholine at nicotinic receptors.
Dose:
0.08 to 0.1 mg/kg IV intubation; 0.01 to 0.015 mg/kg IV maintenance or about 1 mcg/kg/min infusion, titrated to TOF.
Half-life:
About 65 to 75 minutes.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity to vecuronium or bromide formulation components.
Cardiac Considerations:
Minimal direct cardiovascular effect; delayed onset with low cardiac output and prolonged recovery with hepatic disease.
Succinylcholine
MOA:
Depolarizing neuromuscular blocker; activates nicotinic receptors, causing fasciculation followed by flaccid paralysis.
Dose:
0.3 to 1.1 mg/kg IV; usual adult intubating dose 0.6 mg/kg IV. IM dose up to 3 to 4 mg/kg when IV access unavailable.
Half-life:
Very short; rapidly hydrolyzed by plasma pseudocholinesterase, with clinical duration commonly 4 to 6 minutes.
Contraindications:
Malignant hyperthermia history, major burns/trauma after acute phase, denervation/upper motor neuron injury, skeletal muscle myopathy, hyperkalemia risk, or hypersensitivity.
Cardiac Considerations:
Can cause hyperkalemia-related ventricular dysrhythmias/arrest, bradycardia, tachycardia, hypertension, and malignant hyperthermia.

Paralytics Reversal

Sugammadex
MOA:
Selective relaxant binding agent that encapsulates rocuronium or vecuronium, lowering free NMBA concentration and reversing blockade.
Dose:
2 mg/kg IV for T2 reappearance; 4 mg/kg IV for 1 to 2 PTCs; 16 mg/kg IV for immediate reversal after rocuronium 1.2 mg/kg.
Half-life:
About 2 hours with normal renal function; prolonged with renal impairment.
Contraindications:
Known hypersensitivity to sugammadex or formulation components; not recommended with CrCl less than 30 mL/min.
Cardiac Considerations:
Can cause marked bradycardia or cardiac arrest within minutes; monitor hemodynamics and treat significant bradycardia with anticholinergic therapy.
Neostigmine
MOA:
Competitive acetylcholinesterase inhibitor that increases acetylcholine at the neuromuscular junction to overcome nondepolarizing blockade.
Dose:
0.03 to 0.07 mg/kg IV slowly, max 5 mg; give atropine or glycopyrrolate before or with dose and use TOF monitoring.
Half-life:
About 24 to 113 minutes after IV injection; prolonged in anephric patients.
Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity, peritonitis, or mechanical obstruction of the intestinal or urinary tract.
Cardiac Considerations:
Can cause bradycardia, hypotension, or dysrhythmias; use caution with CAD, arrhythmias, or recent ACS and pair with anticholinergic therapy.

Insulin

Regular Insulin
MOA:
Short-acting human insulin that lowers glucose by increasing peripheral glucose uptake and suppressing hepatic glucose production.
Dose:
Individualize to glucose goal and protocol; IV use requires close glucose/potassium monitoring. For hyperkalemia shift, common dose is 10 units IV with dextrose unless hyperglycemic.
Contraindications:
Hypoglycemia or hypersensitivity to regular insulin/formulation components.
Potassium Considerations:
Shifts potassium intracellularly and can cause hypokalemia; monitor and correct potassium, especially with IV insulin or potassium-lowering therapies.

Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitors
Acetazolamide MOA:
Inhibits carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubule, increasing urinary bicarbonate, sodium, water, and potassium loss.
Acetazolamide Dose:
CHF/diuresis: 250 to 375 mg PO/IV once daily in the morning; glaucoma/seizure dosing may range 250 mg to 1 g/day by indication.
Acetazolamide Contraindications:
Hypersensitivity/sulfonamide cross-sensitivity, low sodium or potassium, marked kidney/liver disease, adrenal failure, hyperchloremic acidosis, or cirrhosis.
Acetazolamide Cardiac Considerations:
Can cause metabolic acidosis, hypokalemia, and hyponatremia; monitor electrolytes closely in heart failure, dysrhythmia risk, or QT vulnerability.
Acetazolamide Kidney Considerations:
Avoid in marked kidney disease; renal excretion is primary, nephrolithiasis/crystalluria can occur, and dialysis may help in overdose with renal failure.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor MOA:
Class effect: blocks bicarbonate reabsorption in the proximal nephron, producing alkaline urine and weak diuresis.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor Dose:
Use agent-specific dosing; acetazolamide is commonly 250 to 375 mg daily for diuresis or 250 mg to 1 g/day for other labeled indications.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor Contraindications:
Avoid with significant electrolyte depletion, metabolic acidosis, severe renal/hepatic disease, adrenal failure, or serious sulfonamide reaction history.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor Cardiac Considerations:
Electrolyte loss and acidosis can worsen arrhythmia risk or hemodynamic instability; monitor potassium, sodium, bicarbonate, pH, and volume status.
Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor Kidney Considerations:
Renal function affects safety and efficacy; avoid severe impairment and monitor for worsening renal function, stones, crystalluria, and acid-base shifts.
